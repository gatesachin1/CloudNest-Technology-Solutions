What is Site Reliability Engineering (SRE)?

May 2025 6 min read Cloudnest Team

Site Reliability Engineering — SRE — is a discipline that applies software engineering principles to infrastructure and operations problems. It was invented at Google in 2003 by Ben Treynor Sloss, and has since been adopted by hundreds of companies worldwide to build more reliable, scalable systems.

In plain language: SRE is what happens when a software engineer is given responsibility for keeping production systems running — and uses code to solve problems that would traditionally require manual intervention.

SRE vs. DevOps — What's the Difference?

Both SRE and DevOps aim to break down the wall between development and operations teams. The key difference is in approach:

DevOps

A cultural philosophy and set of practices. DevOps focuses on collaboration, automation, CI/CD, and shortening the feedback loop between development and production.

SRE

An implementation of DevOps with specific prescriptions. SRE defines concrete practices like SLOs, error budgets, and toil reduction — it is more opinionated.

Google's own description: "SRE is what you get when you treat operations as a software problem." If DevOps is the philosophy, SRE is one concrete way to practice it.

The Core Concepts: SLI, SLO, SLA

These three acronyms are the foundation of SRE. Understanding them will transform how you think about system reliability.

SLI — Service Level Indicator

A measurement of how your system is performing. Examples:

  • Request success rate (successful requests / total requests)
  • API latency (percentage of requests under 200ms)
  • Availability (minutes the service was accessible / total minutes)

SLO — Service Level Objective

A target for your SLI. Examples:

  • 99.9% of requests succeed over a 30-day window
  • 95% of API requests complete in under 200ms
  • Website is available 99.95% of the time each calendar month

SLOs are internal targets — they are how your engineering team knows whether the system is healthy. They are slightly stricter than your SLA.

SLA — Service Level Agreement

A contract with customers, with consequences if the target is missed. Most B2B services offer uptime SLAs of 99.9% or 99.95%. If you miss the SLA, you typically owe the customer a credit.

Key insight: Your SLO should be stricter than your SLA. If your SLA promises 99.9% uptime, your internal SLO should target 99.95%. This gives you a buffer so you can detect and fix problems before they breach the SLA and cost you money or reputation.

Error Budgets — The Most Powerful SRE Concept

An error budget is the amount of unreliability your SLO permits. If your SLO is 99.9% availability, you have a 0.1% error budget — roughly 43 minutes of downtime per month.

Error budgets change how teams make decisions:

  • If the error budget is full (plenty remaining): The team can deploy new features aggressively. They have room to take risks.
  • If the error budget is nearly exhausted: Deployments slow down. The team focuses on reliability improvements instead of new features.
  • If the error budget is exhausted: Feature work stops entirely until reliability is restored.

This creates a healthy tension: developers want to ship features, but if they cause too many incidents, their own error budget forces them to stop and fix reliability. No more finger-pointing between "dev" and "ops."

Toil — The Enemy of Reliability

In SRE, toil is manual, repetitive, operational work that scales linearly with service growth and has no lasting value. Examples:

  • Manually restarting a service every time it crashes
  • Manually approving SSL certificate renewals
  • Manually scaling up servers every Friday before a sales event
  • Manually responding to low-priority alerts that aren't actionable

Google's SRE book recommends that SRE teams spend no more than 50% of their time on toil. The rest should be on engineering work — automating away the toil itself, improving reliability, and building better systems.

Practical exercise: List the five most repetitive tasks your operations team does every week. Each one is a toil reduction opportunity — and eliminating them is an SRE project.

Does SRE Apply to Small Businesses?

The full Google-scale SRE model — dedicated SRE teams, complex error budget policies — is overkill for most Indian SMBs. But the principles apply at any scale:

  • Define what "good" looks like — even a simple "our website should be up 99.9% of the time" is a useful SLO.
  • Measure it — set up a free uptime monitor (UptimeRobot, Freshping) and check your availability each month.
  • Automate your most painful manual task — this is toil reduction, even if you don't call it that.
  • Set up meaningful alerts — not "CPU is over 70%" but "error rate is over 1% for 5 minutes."

These four practices will make your business operations more reliable, your team less stressed, and your customers happier — without needing a 50-person SRE organisation.

Summary

SRE is a set of engineering practices — not just a job title — for keeping production systems reliable. Its core ideas are:

  1. Measure reliability with SLIs
  2. Set targets with SLOs
  3. Use error budgets to balance feature velocity and reliability
  4. Eliminate toil through automation
  5. Build on data, not gut feel or blame

Whether you're a single founder with one AWS server, or a 20-person startup with microservices, these principles will help you build and operate software that your customers can depend on.
